当智能不再稀缺：我们为什么还在训练这么多模型？

从 Anthropic、Cursor 到具身智能：技术能力、融资叙事与经济边界

Yaoliang Bian (Jeff) · November 20, 2026 · Personal perspective

← Home中文全文English Full TextSources上一篇：瓶颈将转移到哪里？
技术进步 ≠ 产品价值 ≠ 商业收入 ≠ 资本回报。

01｜模型能力不等于商业价值

过去几年，AI 创业逐渐形成一套熟悉的路径：融资、购买算力、训练模型、刷新 Benchmark、展示 Demo，再以技术进展争取下一轮融资。这不意味着模型研究没有价值，却容易让人忽略一个问题：能力提升是否真的会转化成客户愿意持续付费的结果？

一个针对金融、法律或机器人任务进一步训练的模型，可能显著优于开源基座；但客户真正的替代方案，可能是已经完成大规模通用 Pre-training 和 Post-training 的 Frontier Model，加上检索、工具、记忆、验证与工作流。比较不能只停留在一个排行榜。

02｜垂直领域真的需要额外训练吗？

问题不是“Post-training 和 ICL 哪个更好”。Frontier Model 本身已经经过大量 Post-training。真正的问题是：当充分训练的通用模型进入一个新行业或任务时，额外的 Domain-specific Weight Adaptation 何时值得？

金融与法律常常需要及时、获授权的数据、引用追溯、专业责任和企业系统接口。知识缺失可能先由 Retrieval 与 Tools 解决；若模型在特定决策行为上持续失败，才值得测试进一步 SFT 或 RL。具身智能则还有连续动作、接触动力学和低延迟控制，可能需要训练专门的动作策略，但不必因此默认从零训练整套通用基模。

03｜训练是否值得，是动态的经济问题

在质量和可靠性达到相同要求的前提下，可以用一个最简单的门槛表示专项训练的经济性：

N × (cfrontier − cspecialized) > Ctrain + Cmaintain

这里的 N 是生命周期内任务量，c 是每次任务的期望总成本。这只是决策起点：现实还需包括失败、重试、人工接管、部署、数据授权、硬件折旧和资本成本。尤其重要的是，通用模型的能力和 API 价格会继续变化。因此，一次额外训练的竞争优势可能在收回投入之前就已经折旧。

大市场不是从头 Pre-training 的充分理由。 真正的理由应来自现有模型无法经济补齐的基础能力缺口、合法可用的数据、跨任务复用和可回收的投入。

04｜Anthropic：上市证明需求，不证明股东回报

Anthropic 在 2026 年 11 月 19 日于纳斯达克交易，发行价为 83 美元，首日收于 97.17 美元。根据路透社报道，收盘时完全稀释估值约为 1.77 万亿美元；约 2 万亿美元是盘中触及的量级，不能混为一谈。[1]

根据其 IPO 文件，2026 年前九个月收入为 332.42 亿美元，同时披露了高额计算与基础设施开支及长期合同承诺。[2] 这些数字证明了真实的商业规模，却不能直接证明每一轮 Frontier 训练都能创造足够的投入资本回报。

一套 AI 系统可能为律所节省巨额人力成本，但模型供应商未必能够捕获同等价值。技术价值、社会价值、供应商收入和股东回报，必须分别衡量。

05｜Cursor：工作流与模型供应链的博弈

Cursor 的起点不是自建最强基础模型，而是获得开发者的工作入口：编辑器、项目上下文、工具和 Agent 执行系统。更强的上游模型理论上可以成为产品红利。

但 Cursor 于 2026 年 8 月被 SpaceX 收购，随后 OpenAI 宣布拟终止相关模型供应合同。[3][4] 这一特定合同事件并不证明模型供应商必然封锁应用平台，却说明了依赖上游的战略风险。

Cursor 的路径提示了另一种资本配置顺序：先用产品验证任务和付费需求，再决定哪些模型能力值得自己研发。 但其客户级毛利、不同模型的实际采购成本和长期留存仍缺乏完整公开数据，不能把高收入自动等同于持久护城河。

06｜为什么 VC 可能偏爱模型叙事？

一位投资人曾问我：“如果已有订单和现金流，也不需要烧钱训练模型，为什么还需要融资？除非是扩大规模。”这句话指向的是公司的资金需求与基金的收益目标之间的区别。

模型训练需要的算力、数据和人才很容易形成一套资本需求叙事；真实工作流的长期壁垒——客户复购、低成本交付、数据使用权、执行可靠性——反而需要更长时间验证。这可能令一些资本密集型路线更容易被看见。

但 VC 并非只偏好模型公司，资本密集也不等于低效。需要防止的是把技术复杂性、融资能力和商业护城河当成同一件事。真正有价值的融资，应让新增资本产生足够高的增量回报，而不是把花钱本身变成能力证明。

07｜具身智能：进入工厂不等于改善生产率

机器人能进厂、拿到订单、完成验收，是重要进展；但它们分别不等于连续生产、相对传统自动化的成本优势和规模化复购。面对焊接、标准分拣和固定工位搬运，专机、机械臂与 AMR 往往已经很成熟。

通用机器人可能真正具有增量价值的地方，是传统自动化不值得覆盖的频繁换线、多品种小批量、非标准化操作，以及家庭中的跨场景家务任务。

这个价值最好由三个指标检验：Cost of Adaptation（面对新任务需要多少示教、计算与人工调试），Cost per Successful Operation（每次合格操作的完整成本），以及实际客户复购。单纯将机器人卖给工厂，不能替代这些证据。

08｜从 Demo 到现实，还需要检查评测本身

NVIDIA 的 Astranomy 研究发现，通用 Frontier Model 在 RoboLab-120 仿真任务中可以取得很强的具身表现：其 Direct 和 Agentic 两种方案报告的成功率分别为 66.3% 和 74.8%，但 Agentic 方案平均消耗约 3090 万 Token，估算成本约 36.40 美元/Episode；实验中推理期间仿真暂停。[5]

这不是“通用模型已经取代专用机器人”的证据，而是提醒我们：能力前沿和经济可部署前沿可能相距很远。 仿真结果还依赖动力学、关节约束、控制接口与成功判定的有效性。要判断真实经济边界，需要审计评测、记录全部失败与人工介入，再对关键结论做真机验证。

09｜当智能不再稀缺，什么仍然稀缺？

我越来越倾向于把长期价值放在三件事上：Execution（真正执行任务的权限与能力）、Verification（可靠检验成果的方法）和 Distribution（客户入口与工作流）。但它们也不是天然护城河：工具可以复制，评测可以开源，入口可以迁移。

因此，对一家 AI 公司我会做三个测试：更换底层模型后还剩下多少价值？下一代模型能力提升、价格下降时会受益还是受损？用户入口发生变化时，还拥有哪些不可替代的资源？

模型能力不断提高不意味着所有训练都不值得。 它意味着每一笔领域训练投入，都需要与当时最强的可用替代方案，以及持续变化的技术成本进行比较。

结语｜从 Model-first 到 Value-first

我们的研究问题不应该是“能不能再训练出一个更强的模型”，而应该是：What is the most economical way to turn intelligence into reliable outcomes?

对于金融、法律和具身智能，答案可能不同；有时是额外训练，有时是更好的数据与工具，有时是专用控制器，有时是可靠执行与验证。

五年后，当今天最先进的模型不再先进，一家公司究竟还拥有什么？如果答案只是某个 Checkpoint，它的价值可能很脆弱；如果是持续的客户需求、有效的执行系统、反馈数据和可验证的交付，则更有机会跨越技术周期。

When Intelligence Becomes Cheap, Why Are We Still Training So Many Models?

Technical progress ≠ Product value ≠ Revenue ≠ Capital returns.

01 | Model Capability Is Not Business Value

Over the past few years, AI startups have developed a familiar playbook: raise capital, buy compute, train a model, climb a benchmark, demonstrate impressive capabilities, and use that progress to raise the next round. None of this means model research lacks value. It does raise a question: does better model performance translate into outcomes for which customers will consistently pay?

A domain-adapted model for finance, law, or robotics may dramatically outperform its original open-weight base. Yet its commercial alternative is often a frontier model that has already undergone extensive general pre-training and post-training, supplemented by retrieval, tools, memory, verification, and a product workflow. Beating the base model is not enough to establish commercial superiority.

02 | Does Every Vertical Need More Training?

This is not a contest between post-training and in-context learning: frontier models themselves have already benefited from extensive post-training. The relevant question is when additional domain-specific weight adaptation is justified as a well-trained general-purpose model enters a new task or industry.

Finance and law frequently demand current and licensed information, attributable evidence, professional accountability, and integration with enterprise systems. Retrieval and tools may solve knowledge gaps more directly than another training run. If systematic failures remain in specialized reasoning or execution, SFT or RL may be warranted. Embodied systems additionally require continuous actions, contact dynamics, and low-latency control, which may justify a separately trained action policy without requiring a complete new general-purpose foundation model.

03 | Training Economics Change Over Time

When two approaches meet the same quality and reliability constraints, a simple test for the economic merits of specialized training is:

N × (cfrontier − cspecialized) > Ctrain + Cmaintain

Here N is the number of tasks over the chosen lifecycle and c is the expected all-in cost of one task. This is only a starting point. Failures, retries, human takeovers, deployment, licensed data, depreciation, and the cost of capital also matter. Crucially, frontier capability and API prices evolve. An advantage bought through training may depreciate before its cost is recovered.

A large addressable market is not, by itself, a reason to pre-train from scratch. That requires a foundational capability gap not economically covered by existing models and tools, usable data rights, transfer across tasks, and a plausible return on the investment.

04 | Anthropic: A Public Listing Validates Demand, Not Returns

Anthropic began trading on Nasdaq on November 19, 2026, at an offering price of $83 per share and closed its first day at $97.17. Reuters reported a roughly $1.77 trillion fully diluted valuation at the close; the approximately $2 trillion figure referred to an intraday peak, not the closing valuation.[1]

Its IPO disclosures reported approximately $33.24 billion in revenue for the first nine months of 2026, alongside substantial infrastructure-related spending and long-term commitments.[2] This reflects substantial real commercial demand. It does not establish that every new round of frontier-model investment will earn an adequate return on invested capital.

An AI service could save a law firm millions in labor costs while the model supplier captures only a fraction of that value. Technical progress, social value, supplier revenue, and shareholder returns must be evaluated separately.

05 | Cursor: The Contest for Workflow and Model Supply

Cursor did not begin by building the strongest foundation model. It entered through the developer's workflow: the editor, repository context, tools, and agent execution. In principle, progress in upstream models makes its product more useful.

Cursor joined SpaceX in August 2026. OpenAI subsequently announced plans to end the associated model-supply arrangement.[3][4] That particular contractual dispute does not prove that model providers will universally shut out application companies. It does expose a strategic dependency on upstream suppliers.

The alternative order of capital allocation is compelling: validate customer needs through a product, then decide which model capabilities are worth building in-house. Still, comprehensive public data on Cursor's customer-level contribution margins, model procurement costs, and retention is unavailable. Revenue scale alone is not evidence of a durable moat.

06 | Why Might VCs Prefer a Model Narrative?

An investor once asked me: “If you already have orders and cash flow, and you don't need to burn money training a model, why raise money at all—unless you're scaling?” The question distinguishes a company's need for capital from a venture fund's return objective.

Compute, data, and talent make a model-training plan easy to turn into a capital-requirements story. Durable workflow advantages—customer renewal, low-cost delivery, data rights, and operational reliability—may take much longer to verify. This can make capital-intensive technological narratives more legible to some investors.

But VCs do invest in applications, and capital intensity is not intrinsically wasteful. Technical difficulty, fundraising capacity, and commercial defensibility are distinct concepts. Good financing should convert incremental capital into disproportionate long-term enterprise value; spending itself is not proof of a moat.

07 | In a Factory, Deployment Is Not Productivity

A robot entering a factory, receiving an order, or passing an acceptance test is meaningful. None alone demonstrates sustained production, a cost advantage over conventional automation, or repeat purchasing at scale. Welding, standard parcel sorting, and fixed-station handling already have highly optimized special-purpose machines, industrial arms, and mobile robots.

The more promising incremental market for general-purpose robotics may lie in tasks that existing automation cannot economically cover: frequent changeovers, high-mix low-volume production, nonstandard manipulation, and variable household chores.

Three measures matter: cost of adaptation (new demonstrations, compute, and human engineering needed for a task change), cost per successful operation (complete cost per acceptable result), and actual repeat purchases. Shipping robots to factories does not substitute for these measurements.

08 | From Demos to Reality: Audit the Evaluation

NVIDIA's Astranomy reported strong embodied capability for a general-purpose frontier model on the simulated RoboLab-120 suite. Direct and agentic configurations achieved reported success rates of 66.3% and 74.8%, respectively, but the agentic approach used about 30.9 million tokens per episode on average, with an estimated cost of $36.40 per episode; the simulator paused during inference.[5]

This does not demonstrate that a general-purpose model has replaced specialized robot policies. It reveals the distance between a capability frontier and an economically deployable frontier.

Simulated results also depend on valid physics, joint limits, control interfaces, and success checks. Real-world economic comparisons need evaluation audits, the cost of failed attempts and human intervention, and physical-robot validation for critical findings.

09 | If Intelligence Becomes Cheap, What Remains Scarce?

I increasingly think about three sources of enduring value: execution (permission and capacity to complete work in the world), verification (reliable ways to establish whether the work succeeded), and distribution (customer access and placement inside workflows). None is an automatic moat: tools can be copied, evaluations can be open-sourced, and user interfaces can migrate.

I would ask three questions of an AI company. How much product value survives a switch in the underlying model? Does the business gain or lose when a new model becomes ten times more capable and cheaper? What defensible assets remain if the primary user interface changes?

Cheaper intelligence does not make all training unnecessary. It raises the burden of proof for each additional domain-specific training investment relative to the best available alternative and a rapidly changing cost curve.

Conclusion | From Model-First to Value-First

The central question should not simply be whether we can train another stronger model. It should be: What is the most economical way to turn intelligence into reliable outcomes?

The answer will differ between finance, law, and robotics. Sometimes the right investment is in weight adaptation; sometimes in licensed data, tools, verification, a specialized controller, or an execution system.

Five years from now, when today's best models are no longer the best, what will a company still own? If the answer is only a checkpoint, its value may be fragile. Recurring demand, execution capabilities, proprietary feedback, and verifiable delivery stand a better chance of outliving a model generation.
